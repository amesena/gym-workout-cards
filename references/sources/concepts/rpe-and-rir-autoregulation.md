# RPE and RIR Autoregulation

RPE (Rating of Perceived Exertion) based on RIR (Reps in Reserve) is a framework developed and popularized by Mike Tuchscherer and modified for strength/hypertrophy by Eric Helms (Helms et al., pp. 45–50).

## The RIR-Based RPE Scale

| RPE Rating | Reps in Reserve (RIR) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **10** | 0 RIR | Maximum effort, no additional reps possible |
| **9.5** | 0–1 RIR | Could not do another rep, but could increase weight slightly |
| **9** | 1 RIR | 1 rep remaining |
| **8.5** | 1–2 RIR | Definitely 1 rep left, maybe 2 |
| **8** | 2 RIR | 2 reps remaining |
| **7.5** | 2–3 RIR | Definitely 2 reps left, maybe 3 |
| **7** | 3 RIR | 3 reps remaining (speed work / light warm-up) |

## Benefits of Autoregulation

1. **Accounting for Daily Fatigue**: Absolute strength fluctuates daily due to sleep, nutrition, and stress. Fixed percentage loading (% 1RM) can force a lifter into unsafe effort on high-fatigue days or under-load them on high-readiness days (Helms et al., p. 47).
2. **Targeting Optimal Hypertrophic Effort**: Prescribing 1–3 RIR guarantees that sets hit the target motor unit recruitment threshold without inducing excessive central fatigue (Helms et al., pp. 48–49; [[concepts/proximity-to-failure]]).

## Application in Hypertrophy Programming

- **Target RIR Range**: 1 to 3 RIR across standard mesocycles, reaching 0–1 RIR during the final week prior to a deload (Helms et al., p. 50).
- **RIR Stop Rules**: Terminating a set when RIR drops below a threshold (e.g., stopping when speed slows to 1 RIR) limits cumulative systemic fatigue.
